\part{Project Overview}
\section{Research Question}

The central scientific question of this thesis is: 

\emph{When do flexible innovation models materially improve probabilistic forecast calibration under innovation misspecification?}

This thesis studies how assumptions regarding the innovation process affect probabilistic forecasting performance under non-Gaussian and distributionally shifted stochastic environments. Classical forecasting systems often rely on Gaussian or simple parametric innovation assumptions, which may fail under heavy tails, skewness, heteroskedasticity, structural breaks, or regime-dependent behavior. The primary methodological contribution of this work is diffusion-based innovation modeling, where flexible generative models are used to learn empirical innovation distributions directly from forecast residuals. Robustness enters as a secondary component through Wasserstein-based perturbation analysis, allowing the sensitivity and stability of probabilistic forecasts to be evaluated under controlled distributional shifts.

\section{Core Workflow}

The proposed framework begins with observed multivariate time-series data arising either from simulated stochastic systems or real financial forecasting environments. Simulated systems are used to construct controlled forecasting environments with known innovation structures, while real-data applications provide empirical validation under naturally occurring non-Gaussian behavior. Forecasting systems considered in this thesis include both a classical Vector Autoregression (VAR) model and a modern deep-learning forecasting framework based on DeepAR.

For the classical forecasting setting, the VAR model is defined as

$$
y_t
=
A_1 y_{t-1}
+
A_2 y_{t-2}
+
\cdots
+
A_p y_{t-p}
+
u_t,
$$

where $u_t$ denotes the innovation process. In parallel, a DeepAR-style recurrent probabilistic forecasting model is used as a deep-learning benchmark capable of modeling conditional predictive distributions through recurrent neural network dynamics.

After fitting the forecasting system, residuals are extracted and treated as empirical innovation proxies. Specifically, residuals are computed as

$$
\hat{u}_t
=
y_t - \hat{y}_t,
$$

where $\hat{y}_t$ denotes the fitted forecast from the forecasting model. These residuals are not assumed to represent the true latent innovation process exactly, but instead serve as observable approximations of the innovations driving the stochastic system.

The primary methodological contribution of the thesis is to model the innovation distribution directly using diffusion-based generative models. Rather than assuming

$$
u_t
\sim
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),
$$

the innovation process is learned flexibly from the empirical residual distribution using denoising diffusion and score-matching methods. The diffusion model is trained through a forward noising process of the form

$$
q(x_t \mid x_0)
=
\mathcal{N}
\left(
\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0,
(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)I
\right),
$$

while a neural network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$ is trained to estimate the injected noise term through denoising score matching. Once trained, the diffusion model generates synthetic innovation samples that represent simulated future shocks.
These generated innovations are recursively propagated through the forecasting system in order to generate probabilistic forecast trajectories. In the VAR setting, probabilistic forecasts are generated through recursive simulation according to

$$
y_{t+h}
=
A_1 y_{t+h-1}
+
\cdots
+
A_p y_{t+h-p}
+
u_{t+h}^{(s)},
$$

where $u_{t+h}^{(s)}$ denotes a sampled innovation from the learned diffusion-based innovation distribution. Repeated Monte Carlo simulation produces predictive distributions, forecast trajectories, uncertainty bands, and tail-risk scenarios.

The resulting forecast distributions are evaluated primarily through probabilistic calibration and uncertainty quality rather than point forecasting accuracy alone. Calibration quality is evaluated using Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Probability Integral Transform (PIT) diagnostics, predictive interval coverage, and calibration curves. Probabilistic forecast quality is further evaluated using energy scores, Continuous Ranked Probability Scores (CRPS), and log predictive density. These metrics allow the thesis to systematically compare diffusion-based innovation models against Gaussian, bootstrap, and heavy-tailed innovation assumptions under varying forms of innovation misspecification.

To evaluate robustness under distributional uncertainty, the learned innovation distribution and generated forecast trajectories are subjected to Wasserstein-based perturbation analysis. Specifically, perturbations are introduced either through direct trajectory perturbation or through perturbations of the learned innovation distribution within Wasserstein ambiguity sets of the form

$$
\mathcal{U}_\epsilon(\hat{P})
=
\left\{
Q
:
W(Q,\hat{P})
\le
\epsilon
\right\},
$$

where $W(\cdot,\cdot)$ denotes the Wasserstein distance and $\hat{P}$ denotes the learned innovation distribution. The objective is to evaluate forecast sensitivity, robustness degradation, calibration stability, and tail-risk behavior under controlled perturbations and stress scenarios.

\section{Future Extension}

A natural future extension of this work is adaptive recursive robust diffusion forecasting. In the current framework, the innovation distribution is learned globally from residual data and remains fixed during forecast generation. A more advanced extension would instead learn conditional innovation distributions of the form

$$
p(u_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_t),
$$

where $\mathcal{F}_t$ denotes the current forecast state, latent regime information, volatility conditions, or recent innovation history. Such a framework would allow innovations to adapt dynamically to changing system conditions, resulting in adaptive recursive probabilistic forecasting systems capable of state-dependent uncertainty generation and robustness-aware trajectory simulation.

\section{Summary}
When do flexible innovation models materially improve probabilistic forecast calibration under innovation misspecification?

\begin{enumerate}
\item Start with observed time series (financial returns or simulated data) and fit a VAR and DeepAR
\item Extract residuals treated as empirical innovation proxies
\item Train a diffusion model on the residual distribution via denoising diffusion/score matching
\item Sample new innovations from the diffusion model, which become simulated future shocks
\item Feed sampled innovations back into the forecasting recursion
\item Generate probabilistic forecast trajectories
\item Evaluate calibration and forecast quality using ECE, PIT, and energy scores
\item Perturb the learned distribution or generated trajectories and evaluate tail sensitivity and robustness degradation
\end{enumerate}
Future extensions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item extend the project to adaptive recursive robust diffusion forecasting
    \item apply to medical imaging through the lenses of stochastic reconstruction error
\end{itemize}
\part{Innovation Misspecification and Motivation}

\section{Controlled Innovation Misspecification Environments}

The first stage of the project constructs controlled multivariate stochastic environments designed to isolate the effects of innovation misspecification in probabilistic forecasting systems. The primary objective is not yet to improve forecasting performance, but rather to create settings where classical Gaussian innovation assumptions either succeed or fail in identifiable and measurable ways.

Throughout all experiments, the autoregressive dynamics are held fixed while the innovation distributions are varied. This separation is important because it allows differences in forecast uncertainty behavior to be attributed primarily to the innovation process rather than to changes in the temporal dynamics themselves.

We consider a stable VAR$(1)$ process of the form

$$
y_t = A y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t,
$$

where $y_t \in \mathbb{R}^k$ is a multivariate time series and $\varepsilon_t$ denotes the innovation process. All simulated systems share the same coefficient matrix

$$
A =
\begin{bmatrix}
-0.3747 & -0.1393 & 0.4878 \\
0.0735 & 0.3486 & 0.2186 \\
-0.2411 & 0.2053 & -0.1199
\end{bmatrix},
$$

with innovation covariance matrix

$$
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
1.0 & 0.3 & 0.1 \\
0.3 & 1.0 & 0.2 \\
0.1 & 0.2 & 1.0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The coefficient matrix was generated to satisfy stability conditions so that all simulated systems remain stationary. As a result, the primary source of variation across experiments arises from the innovation distribution itself.

Four innovation regimes were constructed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Gaussian innovations,
    \item heavy-tailed Student-$t$ innovations,
    \item Gaussian mixture innovations,
    \item heteroskedastic innovations with time-varying volatility.
\end{enumerate}

The Gaussian system serves as a benchmark setting in which classical assumptions are approximately correct. The remaining systems are designed to introduce progressively stronger forms of innovation misspecification including heavy tails, asymmetric shock behavior, rare extreme events, and volatility clustering.

For the Student-$t$ system, innovations were generated with low degrees of freedom in order to induce substantial tail behavior. The mixture system introduces occasional high-variance shocks through a two-component Gaussian mixture process. The heteroskedastic system periodically varies the innovation scale over time, producing clustered volatility behavior similar to that observed in many financial and macroeconomic time series.

A total of $n = 400$ observations were simulated for each system after burn-in removal, with dimensionality $k = 3$. Figure~\ref{fig:dgp_series} displays the first simulated series from each innovation regime.

The Gaussian system exhibits relatively stable fluctuations around a constant variance level. In contrast, the Student-$t$ and mixture systems display intermittent extreme shocks and visibly heavier tails. The heteroskedastic system exhibits periods of elevated and reduced volatility, producing clear volatility clustering over time.

\section{VAR Forecasting Backbone and Residual Innovation Analysis}

After constructing controlled innovation misspecification environments, the next stage of the project investigates how these innovation structures behave after fitting a classical forecasting model. The primary goal is to determine whether non-Gaussian innovation behavior remains visible in residual-based innovation proxies after model fitting.

To isolate the role of innovation modeling itself, the same forecasting backbone is used across all simulated systems. In particular, a VAR$(1)$ model is fit independently to each simulated dataset using ordinary least squares estimation. The forecasting equation is given by

$$
y_t = \hat{A} y_{t-1} + \hat{\varepsilon}_t,
$$

where $\hat{A}$ denotes the estimated autoregressive coefficient matrix and $\hat{\varepsilon}_t$ represents the fitted residual innovations.

The estimated residuals are of central importance because they serve as empirical innovation proxies for all subsequent probabilistic forecasting experiments. Rather than modeling the true latent innovations directly, the project studies the distributions that can be recovered from fitted forecasting systems.

For all simulated systems, the fitted VAR models remained stable. The estimated companion matrix eigenvalues satisfied $\max_i |\lambda_i| < 1,$
ensuring stationary forecast dynamics across all experiments. This is important because it guarantees that subsequent differences in probabilistic forecast behavior arise primarily from innovation modeling rather than from unstable autoregressive dynamics.

\section{Classical Innovation Models and Their Limitations}

The previous sections established that several simulated systems retain substantial non-Gaussian structure even after fitting stable VAR forecasting models. The next question is therefore whether standard innovation modeling approaches can adequately recover these residual innovation distributions.
To investigate this question, three classical innovation models were fit to the extracted VAR residuals:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Gaussian innovation models,
    \item bootstrap innovation models,
    \item parametric Student-$t$ innovation models.
\end{enumerate}

These innovation models represent common approaches for generating probabilistic forecast shocks in classical forecasting systems.
Let $\{\hat{\varepsilon}_t\}_{t=1}^n$ denote the fitted VAR residuals. The Gaussian innovation model assumes

$$
\hat{\varepsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}, \hat{\Sigma}),
$$

where $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\Sigma}$ are estimated directly from the residual sample. Under this framework, probabilistic forecast trajectories are generated by repeatedly sampling Gaussian innovations from the fitted covariance structure.
The bootstrap innovation model instead samples directly from the empirical residual distribution,

$$
\hat{\varepsilon}_t^\ast \sim \{\hat{\varepsilon}_1, \ldots, \hat{\varepsilon}_n\},
$$

thereby preserving the observed empirical residual structure without imposing a parametric distributional assumption.
The Student-$t$ innovation model introduces heavier tails through the multivariate Student-$t$ distribution,

$$
\hat{\varepsilon}_t \sim t_\nu(\hat{\mu}, \hat{\Sigma}),
$$

where $\nu$ denotes the degrees of freedom parameter controlling tail heaviness.
For each simulated system, all three innovation models were fit using the same residual samples extracted from the fitted VAR systems. Monte Carlo innovation trajectories were then generated from each fitted innovation model for comparison against the empirical residual distributions.

While this may appear visually stable, it also demonstrates an important limitation: Gaussian innovations can underestimate tail risk by collapsing complex residual structure into second-moment approximations alone.
The bootstrap innovation model preserves empirical nonlinearities more effectively, particularly in the mixture and heteroskedastic systems. However, because bootstrap innovations are constrained to previously observed residual realizations, the method lacks the ability to generalize beyond the empirical sample and may inadequately represent rare or previously unseen shock behavior.
The Student-$t$ innovation model captures heavier tails more aggressively but imposes a rigid parametric structure that can substantially distort the empirical innovation geometry. In several systems, the Student-$t$ samples exhibit tail amplification that exceeds the observed residual behavior.

These observations suggest that the challenge of innovation modeling is not merely the introduction of heavier tails, but rather the ability to flexibly learn complex residual distributions while preserving realistic uncertainty structure.
This distinction is especially important for the mixture and heteroskedastic systems. In the mixture regime, the residual distribution exhibits nonlinear tail curvature and asymmetric shock behavior that cannot be adequately represented by Gaussian covariance structure alone. In the heteroskedastic regime, the residual distribution contains strong time-varying volatility behavior that is poorly approximated by static parametric innovations.

The limitations observed in all three classical innovation models motivate the introduction of flexible generative innovation models capable of learning richer residual structures directly from the data. Rather than imposing fixed parametric forms or relying solely on empirical resampling, diffusion-based generative models provide a framework for learning complex innovation distributions through iterative denoising and score-based density estimation.

The next section introduces diffusion-based innovation modeling as a flexible generative framework for residual innovation learning and probabilistic forecast generation.

\section{Motivation for Diffusion-Based Innovation Modeling}

Diffusion models provide a flexible nonparametric framework for learning complex probability distributions through iterative denoising. Rather than specifying a fixed parametric innovation family, diffusion models learn how to progressively transform simple noise distributions into samples that approximate the empirical residual distribution.

Let $x_0$ denote a residual innovation sample. The forward diffusion process progressively corrupts the residual through Gaussian perturbations,

$$
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})
=
\mathcal{N}
\left(
x_t;
\sqrt{1 - \beta_t} x_{t-1},
\beta_t I
\right),
$$

where $\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^T$ denotes a variance schedule controlling the noise injection process.

After sufficiently many diffusion steps, the residual distribution approaches an approximately Gaussian latent distribution. A neural network is then trained to reverse this process by learning how to remove noise from partially corrupted residual samples.

The reverse diffusion process is parameterized as

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t),
$$

where the neural network parameters $\theta$ are optimized using denoising score matching objectives. Intuitively, the model learns the geometry of the residual innovation distribution by repeatedly predicting and removing injected noise.

In this project, diffusion models are trained directly on residual innovation proxies extracted from the fitted VAR systems. The objective is therefore not unconditional image generation or sequence generation, but rather flexible innovation distribution learning for probabilistic forecasting.
This distinction is important. The diffusion model is not replacing the forecasting backbone itself. Instead, the forecasting architecture remains fixed while the innovation-generation mechanism becomes more flexible.

The forecasting workflow therefore becomes

$$
\text{Data}
\rightarrow
\text{Fit}
\rightarrow
\text{Error Extraction}
\rightarrow
\text{Learning}
\rightarrow
\text{Forecast}.
$$

Under this framework, innovations sampled from the learned diffusion model can be recursively propagated through the forecasting system to generate probabilistic forecast trajectories.

Conceptually, diffusion innovation modeling offers several advantages over the classical innovation approaches examined previously:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item it does not impose a fixed parametric innovation family,
    \item it can learn nonlinear residual geometry directly from empirical data,
    \item it can represent multimodal and asymmetric innovation structure,
    \item it can potentially adapt to complex tail behavior without explicit tail parameterization.
\end{enumerate}

These properties are especially important in the mixture and heteroskedastic systems constructed earlier. In those regimes, the residual distributions contain structures that are difficult to represent through Gaussian covariance matrices or static heavy-tail parameters alone.

The remainder of the project investigates whether diffusion-based innovation models can improve probabilistic forecast calibration relative to classical innovation assumptions. In particular, the next stage evaluates whether diffusion-generated innovations produce more realistic forecast distributions under innovation misspecification environments involving heavy tails, volatility clustering, and rare extreme shocks.
